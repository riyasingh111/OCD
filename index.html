OCD Self-Check Calculator (OCI-R)

Do unwanted thoughts or repeated habits take up a lot of your day? This free tool uses the 18-question OCI-R, a scale used by doctors and researchers, to turn your answers into a simple score.[1] It is a self-check, not a diagnosis. Only a doctor or mental health professional can diagnose OCD, and effective treatment exists.[2][3] Your answers stay on your device and are never sent anywhere.

Your 18-question self-check

What is OCD? What is the OCI-R? tap to read

OCD (obsessive-compulsive disorder) has two parts. Obsessions are unwanted thoughts, images or urges that keep coming back and cause distress. Compulsions are things you feel you must do again and again, such as washing, checking, counting or arranging, to ease that distress. Many people have odd unwanted thoughts now and then.[4] It becomes a health problem when the thoughts and rituals cause real distress or get in the way of daily life.

The OCI-R is a short list of 18 statements. You say how much each one has bothered you in the past month, from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). We add up your answers and compare them with cut-off scores found in research.[5]

How do I answer? tap to read

What is OCD?

Think of OCD as a loop. An unwanted thought shows up and feels alarming. Doing a ritual brings a little relief, but the relief does not last, so the thought comes back and the loop starts again. Treatment works by breaking the loop.[2][3]

OCD facts: common worries answered

Tap a question to read the answer. 🔊 reads it out loud.

Are unwanted thoughts normal?

Yes, many people without OCD also have strange or unwanted thoughts.[4] A thought is not an action, and having one does not mean you want it to happen. It becomes a problem when it causes a lot of distress and takes over your time.

Is OCD only about washing and cleaning?

No. OCD can be about checking, putting things in order, counting or repeating, unwanted thoughts, or keeping too many things. This self-check looks at six of these areas.

Can OCD be treated?

Yes. Talking therapy called cognitive behavioural therapy, usually with a method called exposure and response prevention, helps many people. Medicines can also help, and some people use both. Your doctor will guide you.[2][3]

Why do people wait so long for help?

Reviews find that shame, not recognising the signs and long waiting lists are common reasons. Many people wait years. People who knew about OCD, or whose family and friends encouraged them, sought help sooner.[6]

How common is OCD, and how long do people wait?

Experts put the lifetime rate worldwide at about 2 to 3 in 100 people. India's 2015 to 2016 national survey found a lower figure of 0.76%, which the authors think may be too low because of stigma.[7] People often wait a long time for help. Reviews report delays of more than 7 years between first symptoms and proper treatment, and one scoping review of 51 studies found that shame, not understanding OCD and long waits for services were common reasons.[6] Knowing the signs and support from family or friends helped people seek care sooner.[6]

How your score is worked out

This calculator uses the OCI-R scoring.[1] In simple steps:

  1. Each answer is worth 0 to 4 points.
  2. The 15 questions about washing, checking, ordering, unwanted thoughts and counting or repeating are added up. This is your OCD-type score, out of 60.
  3. The 3 hoarding questions are added up separately. This is your hoarding score, out of 12.
  4. Each score is compared with a research cut-off: 12 for the OCD-type score and 6 for the hoarding score.[5]

OCD-type score = washing + checking + ordering + obsessing + neutralizing (0 to 60). Hoarding score = hoarding (0 to 12). Total = all 18 answers (0 to 72).

In a study of 474 people, the cut-off of 12 on the OCD-type score picked out people with OCD with about 82% sensitivity and 83% specificity. The hoarding cut-off of 6 did so with about 92% and 93%.[5] In plain words, roughly 1 in 6 people without OCD still score at or above 12, and roughly 1 in 6 people with OCD score below it. That is why the score is only a pointer, not a diagnosis.

What can help

Tap a topic. Help comes in several forms, and many people use more than one.

Therapy: face it step by step

The main talking therapy is cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), usually with exposure and response prevention (ERP). You meet a trigger in small steps and practise not doing the ritual. A 2026 analysis of 61 trials with 3,710 patients found that every CBT format beat comparison groups. Individual, remote and family-involved CBT worked better than group CBT.[2]

A trained therapist builds the steps with you.

Medicine: only with a doctor

Doctors often use medicines called SSRIs. A 2021 analysis of 11 trials found that benefit rose up to about 40 mg of fluoxetine-equivalent a day, while drop-outs from side effects grew at higher doses.[8] Adding ERP to medicine worked better than medicine alone across 21 trials with 1,113 patients.[3] Never start, stop or change a medicine on your own.

Online and self-help programmes

Across 17 studies, about 82% of adults were satisfied with online CBT. About 28% did not finish it, and programmes with a clinician were better liked than self-guided ones.[9] Unguided self-help gave smaller benefits than guided help, but they were still moderate.[2] If you try a programme, tell your doctor.

Family and friends

Families often help by giving reassurance or waiting while a ritual finishes. In a 2025 review of 39 studies, about 95% of families did this at least monthly, and nearly half did so daily.[10] It comes from love. Ask the treating team how to support without taking part in rituals.

Body and mind

Exercise is not a proven treatment for OCD. A 2023 review of six small studies saw large drops in symptoms after exercise, but most had no comparison group.[11] A 2026 review of controlled trials found no clear effect on OCD.[12] Enjoy movement for mood and health, as an extra and not a replacement for treatment.
